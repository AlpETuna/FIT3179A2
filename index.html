Homes, households & the cost of belonging

A place to
call home.

A house, a flat, a room to yourself. A home can take many forms. So can the cost of keeping it.

Two lenses on housing. The 2021 Census gives the latest complete picture of every region. Monthly prices to July 2026 and rent-assistance records from March 2026 bring the story up to date.

01 / The homes

One country.
Many ways to live.

Start at the front door. In the 2021 Census, 30.6% of occupied homes were rented: 2.8 million households. If you rent, you are in nearly one home in three, and your chances depend on the kind of building you live in.

The shape of a building tells only part of the story. The next question is where that home stands.

02 / The places

Same country.
Different rental markets.

Australia’s median weekly rent was $375 in 2021: half of renting households paid less, half paid more. But where you live decides what you pay. The same national figure covers $80 a week in remote Arnhem Land and $720 in Manly.

What is a “region”? The Census groups nearby suburbs and towns into areas of about 30,000 to 130,000 people. The first map compares 335 of them. The second places one dot for every 1,000 rented homes.

Read area and people separately.

A large inland region covers more of the map, but may contain fewer rented homes than a small city region. That is why colour shows local prices in one map, while dots show rented homes in the next.

The histogram adds another perspective: how common each regional median is. Each region counts once, regardless of its population.

Every map uses an Albers equal-area projection, so areas can be compared fairly. The city maps in Figure 05 are enlarged; their 10 km bars show the scale.

03 / The trade-offs

A rent figure is
only half the picture.

The same rent can buy a very different life. Melbourne City and Casey – South charge almost the same, yet one is a city of apartments with lower incomes and the other a suburb of family houses. Bendigo is cheaper, and so are its incomes.

Three Victorian communities: an inner city, an outer suburb and a regional centre. Every other region stays in grey for context.

Local incomes and housing costs vary together. To understand financial pressure, we need a measure calculated from the same household’s income and rent.

04 / The pressure

Help with the rent.
Pressure that remains.

Since the Census, rents have kept climbing: up 9.8% between April 2024 and July 2026. For the 1.4 million renters who receive Commonwealth Rent Assistance, the question is simple: how much of their income is left after rent?

Who is a “recipient”? A single person or a couple, with any children, who receives Commonwealth Rent Assistance with their pension, allowance or family payment. Rental stress means paying more than 30% of income in rent.

44.0%

still in rental stress
with assistance.

Across Australia in March 2026, the share was 75.1% when calculated without assistance, and 44.0% after subtracting it from rent.

Assistance lifts about 430,000 recipients out of rental stress. It still leaves 608,000 paying more than 30% of their income in rent.

Putting the pieces together

608,000 renters,
still in stress after help.

Nearly one home in three is rented, and where it stands sets the price: from $80 a week in Arnhem Land to $720 in Manly. Since 2024 rents have risen faster than prices in general.

Rent Assistance makes a real difference, cutting rental stress among recipients from 75% to 44%. But that 44% is 608,000 renters, and 264,000 of them still hand over more than half their income in rent.

About the story & the data

Sources & dates

  • ABS 2021 Census General Community Profiles: G02, G36, G37 and G42. Census night: 10 August 2021.
  • ABS ASGS 2021 boundaries, simplified for display.
  • ABS monthly Consumer Price Index, July 2026, Table 3.
  • AIHW Commonwealth Rent Assistance, March 2026, CRA.2–3. AIHW analysis of Department of Social Services administrative data in the Australian Government Housing Dataset, drawing on Services Australia payment records.

Methods note

The Census is a 2021 snapshot; prices and rent-assistance records are more recent and describe different groups, so they are shown side by side, not as cause and effect. The three featured communities illustrate different settings rather than a national sample.

Regions are ABS Statistical Areas Level 3 (SA3). Regional charts show regional medians, each region counting once whatever its population, and omit areas without data, non-geographic areas and Other Territories. Census counts are slightly adjusted to protect privacy, so parts may not add exactly to totals.

In Figure 06 each SA3’s dots are shared among its Statistical Areas Level 1 (about 400 people each) in proportion to their rented homes, then placed at a random point inside that small area. Only the dots are published.

Rent-assistance figures count “income units” (a single person or couple, with any children) with enough data to measure affordability. “With assistance” uses (rent − assistance) ÷ gross income; “without” uses rent ÷ gross income for the same recipients, so it is a second calculation, not an earlier measurement. Price changes are the consumer price index rescaled to April 2024.

Data tables used

Census: G37 tenure by dwelling (Figures 01, 03, 05, 06); G36 dwelling structure (02); G42 household composition (04); G02 medians (05, 07–10). Consumer Price Index: Table 3, original monthly series (11). Rent assistance: AIHW CRA.3 rental stress level (12) and CRA.2 characteristics (13, 14).

Methods, accessibility & acknowledgements

All charts use original Vega or Vega-Lite specifications. Each figure provides its data and readable specification. The three-community controls update the scatterplot and parallel-coordinates view using Vega’s View API. Data tables provide an alternative to hovering and to colour-based reading.
